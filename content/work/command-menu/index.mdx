I rebuilt Linear's command menu from scratch, not all of it but enough to understand how it works. Linear is known for its speed and precision, and in my opinion the command menu is the best example of both, because it's woven through the whole app: it opens from anywhere, what it offers changes with what you're looking at, and it changes on every keystroke without making you wait.

I thought I'd try my hand at it as practice and to learn. This isn't a tutorial, it's what I found taking it apart.

This is my version, and it opens on an issue, PND-18, the way Linear's does when you press ⌘K on one. Try changing its status, assigning it or copying its branch name, then press Backspace on the empty field and the issue drops away, leaving you with the rest of the workspace.

I've never worked at Linear, so I can't tell you what they were thinking, but I can show what I found and why I think they made each choice.

## How I Looked at It

I started with inspect element. I opened the menu and right-clicked a row, and the Elements panel showed most of what I needed: the row's height, the highlight behind it, the padding around the list and the font size and weight.

Motion and the keyboard were harder, and the springs were the hardest part of the whole rebuild to pin down. A spring doesn't show up in the Elements panel, so for those I went to the Sources tab. Linear ships its web client like any other site, so the springs and keyboard rules are sitting in the JavaScript, minified but readable.

## Anatomy

The menu is four parts, each with one job: a title row that holds the context, a field, a list and the frame around them. The frame is 720 wide, never wider than the window minus 32 and never taller than 450. (Mine is narrower, because this column is.)

<Anatomy />
<sub>The menu lifts apart into frame, list, focus, field and context, each labelled with its sizes, then stacks back together. Rows are 46 tall, and so is the field, while the list adds 6 above and below.</sub>

The detail that stood out to me most was the focus. The highlighted row looks like the row with a different background, but when I inspected it the row had no background at all. The highlight is a pseudo-element behind it, inset 2 from the top and bottom with a radius of 8, and the list's 6px padding keeps it off the frame.

I think they did this so the highlight feels like something you're holding. A full-width stripe makes a menu read like a table, but an inset pill reads like something you move with the arrow keys.

The type is small too: the field and the rows are 13px at a weight of 450, group headers are 12px at 500 and keycaps are 11px. The frame itself is set at 15px, but almost nothing inherits it. My guess is that's because you glance at a command menu rather than read it. Smaller type fits more rows into 450px, so what you're after is more likely to be on screen before you need to scroll.

## Styles

While I was in the Elements panel, I noticed the class names: instead of something readable like `.row` or `.menu`, they're short hashes, each holding one property. That's StyleX, Meta's styling library, and Linear only recently moved to it.

They used styled-components for years, and in August they finished moving to StyleX after more than 1,000 pull requests.[^1] Kenneth Skovhus, who led the migration, explained why: runtime CSS-in-JS means "users pay for style generation and rule injection while the client renders."[^2] StyleX does that work at build time instead. By their own measurement, navigating between pages went from injecting hundreds of CSS rules to zero, and on a mid-tier machine it's around 30% faster.[^1]

This was an app-wide change, not something they did for the menu. But the more I thought about it, the more sense it made for a menu in particular, because it changes what it shows on every keystroke and every page while a spring is animating its height. With styled-components, a page of rows the menu hadn't shown yet could mean new styles being generated and injected mid-animation, but with StyleX every style the menu needs is already in the stylesheet before you press ⌘K.

That made me curious about the height, and how the frame knows how tall to be.

## Height

The usual way to animate a menu's height is to measure it: render the list, read its size and animate the frame to match. The catch is that the size is always a frame behind whatever you just typed.

Linear's frame never measures itself, because its height is arithmetic: title, field, padding, rows, padding. Every row type has a fixed height, so the next height is known the moment the list changes, before anything is laid out.

<Height />
<sub>The root, Change status… and Labels frames empty down to their title and field, then grow back to fit their rows. Change status works out to 34 + 46 + 6 + 5 × 46 + 6 = 322, while the root runs well past 450, so it's capped and scrolls.</sub>

Group headers count as 30, and without an issue the title row goes, so the frame loses 34.

I think they did this for the same reason as the styles: nothing should happen after you type that could have happened before, so the height starts moving on the same keystroke that changed the list, not one frame later.

Of everything here, this is the idea I'd most want to take into my own work: give every row a fixed height and work the frame out instead of measuring it. It costs almost nothing, and it's why the frame is never a frame behind the list.

## Motion

There are three springs in their JavaScript, and none of them overshoots. Height uses a stiffness of 20000 and a damping of 500, which is close to instant, done in about a tenth of a second. Opening uses 1500 and 100, and closing 2500 and 100.

<Springs />
<sub>The height, close and open springs trace their progress over 300ms, each marked where it reaches 99%, and opening is the slow one.</sub>

Why no bounce at all? I think it's because the pointer is usually resting on a row, so if the frame overshot, the row under it would slide past and come back, and the highlight would flicker between two rows under a hand that hadn't moved.

Opening scales up from 0.98, while closing goes down to 0.96 on the stiffer spring, so it leaves faster than it arrived.[^3] The way I read it, arriving deserves a moment and leaving doesn't, because once you've picked something the menu's job is done and it gets out of the way.

Opening a page doesn't animate the content at all: the frame dips to 0.98 for 80ms and comes back, and the height spring does the rest. My guess is that a slide or a crossfade would make you wait to read the new list, but a dip says "something changed" in 80ms and the rows are already there.

## Context

Most command menus start from nothing, with an empty field and every command in the app, but Linear's starts from where you are.

Opened on an issue, the menu makes the issue its title, with the identifier dimmed but not the title. Everything in the first group is about that issue, so the group drops its header and lets the title say it.

In my opinion, this is the smartest part of the menu. The most likely thing you want is to do something to the issue in front of you, so those commands come first and the menu never asks which issue you meant.

Backspace with nothing typed takes the issue away, but the menu stays open and the list switches to the workspace: create, navigate and search. In Linear (though not in mine), a small backspace glyph sits after the title while the field is empty, so you know what Backspace will do before you press it.

## Pages

"Change status…" replaces the list instead of opening a submenu. The placeholder becomes the action's own wording ("Change status…", "Set priority to…", "Assign to…"), and the title stays where it is.

I think this is about keeping your eyes still. A submenu would put a second box beside the first and pull you sideways, but replacing the list keeps the field, the title and your hands exactly where they were, so only the rows change.

Statuses take the number keys 1 to 5 and priorities 0 to 4, but only while the field is empty, because once you type, numbers are text. I like this one a lot: a number key is a shortcut right up until it can't be, so you never have to think about which mode you're in.

Picking a status closes the menu, but labels don't: they're the one page where rows toggle, so the menu waits until you press Escape. It's an exception that follows the rule, because the menu closes when you're done and with labels only you know when that is.

Typing at the root searches inside the pages too, so "done" finds Done under "Change status" without opening anything. People and labels wait for three characters before they show up there, so a short query doesn't fill with names. My guess is that a workspace has far more people and labels than statuses, and without the wait, typing "d" would bury Done under every Dan and David.

## Keyboard

<Keymap />

A held arrow stops at the end of the list, but a single press wraps. The difference is the repeat flag on the key event, and it's why holding ↓ never throws you back to the top. Most menus I've used pick one behaviour (always wrap, or never), but Linear reads intent: one press at the bottom means "take me to the top", while a held key means "go as far as you can".

Letter shortcuts in the list are labels, so typing S filters for "status" rather than running it. Shortcuts with ⌘ do run from inside the field, so ⌘. copies the ID without clearing your search first. The way I think about it, anything you could type is text, and anything with ⌘ is a command.

The field keeps focus the whole time. Arrows, Enter and typing all go to the input while the list follows along as its active descendant, and clicking a row doesn't blur the field. If I had to sum up the whole menu in one rule, it would be this one: focus never leaves what you're typing.

## Details

Every placeholder ends in a real ellipsis, not three dots. Empty results say what was missing: "No matching states", "No matching users" and "No matching priorities". Escape closes the whole menu from any depth rather than stepping back, because stepping back is Backspace's job.

None of these are hard to build. My guess is they come from people using their own menu all day and fixing whatever bugs them.

## What I'd Take

The way I read it, most of the menu follows the rule from the height: nothing should happen after you type that could have happened before. The frame's height is a sum worked out before layout, the styles are in the stylesheet before the menu opens, and the first group is already about the issue you opened it on, so the menu never has to ask.

My version only covers part of it, but it was enough to understand how it works. I hope some of these decisions help when you build your own.
