Almost everything I know about building interfaces, I learned for free. Nobody charged me to open the inspector on a site I liked. Nobody charged me to read the source of a component library, or a post where someone explained how they built a menu, or a thread where someone broke an animation down frame by frame.

I think about that a lot, because it didn't have to be that way. Every one of those people could have put what they knew behind a gate. Some of them would have made money doing it. Most of them didn't, and I'm doing this work because of it.

So I believe information should be free, and I mean it less as a slogan than as something I owe back.

## Where I Learned

Most of what I know didn't come from a course. It came from a browser.

I'd find a site with a detail I loved, open the developer tools and read how it worked. I'd find the open source library behind a component and go through it file by file until I understood why it was written that way. I read documentation like it was a book. I watched conference talks from people I'd never meet. I followed people who posted what they were working on and, more importantly, how.

None of that was organized. It was a pile of free things that added up to an education. Every single piece was given away by someone who decided that what they knew was more useful shared than kept.

## A Copy Costs Nothing

The reason I think information should be free starts with something simple. When I give you an idea, I still have it.

<CopiesFigure />
<sub>A copy of an idea costs nothing to make, and it doesn't take anything away from the original.</sub>

Most things don't work like that. If I give you my chair, I no longer have a chair. But if I explain how I time a menu, now we both know. You can explain it to two more people, and they can explain it to two more. Nobody along the way is any poorer for it.

<sub>In *Pay It Forward*, Trevor draws his idea for changing the world on a chalkboard. Help three people, and ask each of them to help three more instead of paying you back.</sub>

Thomas Jefferson put it well, more than two hundred years ago. Someone who gets an idea from you receives it without lessening yours, the same way someone who lights a candle from yours gets light without leaving you in the dark.[^1] That was true with letters and printing presses. On the internet, where the cost of a copy is effectively zero, it's even more true.

When something costs nothing to share and grows in value the more people have it, keeping it locked up is a choice to make the world a bit smaller than it needs to be.

## Who a Gate Keeps Out

A paywall doesn't pick the people who want to learn most. It picks the people who can pay.

<GateFigure />
<sub>A gate doesn't filter for curiosity. It filters for money.</sub>

Those are very different groups. The person who'd get the most out of a good explanation of springs might be a student, or someone in a country where a course costs a month's rent, or someone with a job that has nothing to do with design who's teaching themselves at night. The gate stops them first.

I know the gate gets put there for real reasons. People need to make a living. But every time knowledge goes behind a wall, someone on the other side doesn't learn it, and you never find out what they would have made.

## What Free Doesn't Mean

Free doesn't mean the work has no value. Writing something clear takes time. Building a tool takes longer. The people who do it deserve to be supported, and I think we should support them more than we do.

Stewart Brand said information wants to be free, but the full quote is more interesting. He also said information wants to be expensive, because the right information at the right time can change your life. Both are true at once, and the two pull against each other.[^2]

The way I square that is to separate the knowledge from the work of applying it. What I know about motion should be free. My time, a tool I spent a year on, the hours it takes to apply that knowledge to someone else's product, those can cost something. Selling a product is fine. Charging for your time is fine. Hiding the understanding is the part I don't want to do.

Free also doesn't mean free to take credit. If you learned something from someone, say so. Sharing works because people keep giving, and people keep giving because they're seen. Attribution is how you pay for something that has no price.

## How I Try to Do It

Everything I write here is free to read. There's no email you have to hand over first, and no part two behind a subscription.

Every page also has a plain Markdown version, so you can hand it to an agent and have it apply what's here to whatever you're building. The [skills](https://github.com/raphaelsalaja/skills) I use with coding agents are open source. [userinterface.wiki](https://www.userinterface.wiki/) is free, and it'll stay that way.

If something here helped you, there's a link to buy me a coffee at the bottom of the page. It's optional. That's the point. The support should come after the value, from people who want to give it, not before the value, from everyone who wants to see it.

## Why It Comes Back

The thing I didn't expect is how much of it comes back.

When I share how I built something, people write back. They tell me a better way to do it, or they build something on top of it that I'd never have thought of, and then they share that. The person who learned from a free post five years ago is now writing their own, and I'm learning from them.

Knowledge compounds when it gets passed around, and I'd rather be one of the people passing it.

I learned everything I know because people gave it away. The least I can do is the same.
