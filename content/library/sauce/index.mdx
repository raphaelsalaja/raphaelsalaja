I watched [a video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CEMG7ZZ92bk) about the difference between an outfit that's well put together and an outfit that has sauce. Some people dress well on paper, with everything fitting, everything current and a place on every best-dressed list, but something is still missing. Others wear things I'd never pick, and it looks like it couldn't have been any other way.

The Italians have a word for it, sprezzatura, which means making something difficult look like it took no effort.

I think about this with interfaces all the time.

## Correct on Paper

A product can do everything right, with an 8 point grid, a clean sans serif at a sensible size and a neutral palette with one accent, then rounded corners, soft shadows, a command menu and a bento grid on the landing page. It passes review and nobody could point to a mistake.

It's also the product everyone has seen a hundred times, so it could belong to any company, and with the logo off nobody could say whose it is.

That's the well-dressed version, where every choice was made because it's what good looks like right now. The trouble is that right now belongs to everyone, so the product ends up looking like everyone.

## What Has It

The products with sauce are usually recognisable from a single crop. Teenage Engineering is the obvious one, because the corner of a manual or a single knob is enough to know it. Things has looked like Things for years without ever needing to follow what the rest of the to-do apps were doing, and Panic's apps all feel like they come from the same place.

None of them are neutral and some are loud (Teenage Engineering's orange is not a safe colour). What they share is a style that isn't on loan from the moment: it belongs to them, and they keep wearing it.

That's the part I find interesting: a loud product can have it and a quiet, minimal one can lack it, because what matters is being consistent with itself rather than with the trend.

## Trends Aren't the Problem

Every year has its list, like glass, gradients, bento grids and blur behind everything, but the answer isn't to avoid all of it.

There's a difference between seeing a trend and wearing one. Something that's everywhere can be folded into what a product already does, so it looks like it was always going to be there, or it can be added because it's on the list, and then it's the first thing that looks dated a year later.

The question I ask is whether I'd still want it if nobody else was doing it. If the answer is yes, it doesn't matter where it came from, but if it's no, I'm wearing someone else's outfit.

## Where It Comes From

Sprezzatura is a lie, in the nice sense: it looks effortless because the effort is hidden. The person with sauce spent a long time working out what they like and throwing away everything that isn't it.

Interfaces work the same way, and these are a few things I notice in the ones that have it:

- They say no a lot, so most of the style is in what was left out.
- They repeat themselves, with the same radius, the same curve and the same way of arriving on a screen, until it's recognisable.
- Their references come from outside the feed, from print, hardware, signage or an old manual, so they aren't on the same trend cycle.
- The details agree with each other: the motion feels like the type and the type feels like the colour, so nothing reads as bolted on.
- Breaking the style bothers the people who made it, which is the sign that there is one.

None of this is visible in a single screenshot, because it builds up across the whole product, the way someone's style comes through in a year of photos rather than in one.

## The Average

It's never been easier to be well dressed, because a model will produce a clean, correct, current interface in a minute. That's the average of everything it has seen, which is exactly what correct on paper means.

So correct is cheap now, but a point of view isn't, if it's been held long enough to show up in every decision without anyone thinking about it. That's what people recognise, and it's the one thing they can't get from anyone else.

A well-designed product looks good, and I think a product with sauce looks like someone made it, which is the part I keep thinking about.
