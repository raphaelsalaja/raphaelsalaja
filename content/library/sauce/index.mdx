I watched [a video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CEMG7ZZ92bk) about the difference between an outfit that's well put together and an outfit that has sauce. Some people dress well on paper. Everything fits, everything is current, they're on every best-dressed list. Something is still missing. Other people wear things you'd never pick yourself, and it looks like it couldn't have been any other way.

The Italians have a word for it, sprezzatura. Making something difficult look like it took no effort.

I think about this with interfaces all the time.

## Correct on Paper

You can make a product that does everything right. An 8 point grid. A clean sans serif at a sensible size. A neutral palette with one accent. Rounded corners, soft shadows, a command menu, a bento grid on the landing page. It passes review. Nobody could point to a mistake.

You've also seen it a hundred times. It could belong to any company. Take the logo off and nobody could tell you whose it is.

That's the well-dressed version. Every choice on it was made because it's what good looks like right now. The trouble is that right now belongs to everyone, so the product ends up looking like everyone.

## What Has It

The products with sauce are usually recognizable from a single crop. Teenage Engineering is the obvious one. You can see the corner of a manual or one knob and know. Things has looked like Things for years, and it never needed to follow what the rest of the to-do apps were doing. Panic's apps all feel like they come from the same place.

None of them are neutral. Some of them are loud. Teenage Engineering's orange is not a safe color. What they share is that the style isn't on loan from the moment. It belongs to them, and they keep wearing it.

That's the part I find interesting. A loud product can have it and a quiet, minimal one can lack it. What matters is being consistent with yourself rather than with the trend.

## Trends Aren't the Problem

Glass, gradients, bento grids, blur behind everything. Every year has its list. The answer isn't to avoid all of it.

There's a difference between seeing a trend and wearing one. You can take something that's everywhere and fold it into what you already do, so it looks like it was always going to be there. Or you can add it because it's on the list, and then it's the first thing that looks dated a year later.

The question I ask is whether I'd still want it if nobody else was doing it. If the answer is yes, it doesn't matter where it came from. If the answer is no, I'm wearing someone else's outfit.

## Where It Comes From

Sprezzatura is a lie, in the nice sense. It looks effortless because the effort is hidden. The person with sauce spent a long time working out what they like and throwing away everything that isn't it.

Interfaces work the same way. A few things I notice in the ones that have it:

- They say no a lot. Most of the style is in what was left out.
- They repeat themselves. The same radius, the same curve, the same way of arriving on a screen, until it's recognizable.
- Their references come from outside the feed. Print, hardware, signage, an old manual. Things that aren't on the same trend cycle.
- The details agree with each other. The motion feels like the type, the type feels like the color. Nothing reads as bolted on.
- Breaking the style bothers the people who made it. That's how you know there is one.

You won't see any of this in a single screenshot. It shows up across the whole product, the same way you recognize someone's style across a year of photos rather than in one.

## The Average

It's never been easier to be well dressed. A model will give you a clean, correct, current interface in a minute. That's the average of everything it has seen, which is exactly what correct on paper means.

<sub>A model gives you the middle of everything it has seen. That's correct by definition, and it's the same middle for everyone.</sub>

So correct is cheap now. What isn't cheap is a point of view you've held long enough that it shows up in every decision without you thinking about it. That's what people recognize, and it's the one thing they can't get from anyone else.

A well-designed product looks good. A product with sauce looks like someone made it.
