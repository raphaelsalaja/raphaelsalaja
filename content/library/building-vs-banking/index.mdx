On my flight back from San Francisco, I sat near a group coming home from YC's AI Startup School and expected to hear about what they were building.

For most of the flight they talked about valuations: rounds, exits and what a company at their stage could be worth. I didn't hear anyone describe their product or who it was for.

Money isn't the problem, because it takes money to build almost anything, and wanting to be paid well for good work is fine. The trouble starts when it's the only reason, because then the work is just what has to be done to get to the money, and every day spent on it is a cost.

<PayoffFigure />
<sub>Both lines draw out from day one to years later. Building for the work fills in every day along the way, while building for the exit leaves every day empty but the last, ringed with a dashed circle because it might never come.</sub>

It also changes what failure means. Someone who built because they liked the work still has the work, but if the exit was the point and it never comes, there's very little to show for the years.

Most of the people I admire built things because they wanted them to exist: they liked making them and hoped someone else would like them too, and when someone did, that was the reason to make the next one.

<ThreadFigure />
<sub>Building draws as a loop from make it to someone loves it to make more and back round, while banking runs in a straight line from raise to grow to exit, and stops.</sub>

I still think that's the better reason to start something, and the one more likely to end with something people want.
