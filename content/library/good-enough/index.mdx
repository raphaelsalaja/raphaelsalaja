"Good enough" is a useful phrase and a dangerous one: useful when deciding whether to ship, but dangerous when it becomes the standard for the parts people touch every day.

I hear it in two very different situations, and the same words mean opposite things in each one.

## Where It Works

I don't think everything needs to be perfect. Most decisions in a product are reversible, and waiting on them is how nothing gets finished, so the flow and the empty state can ship and the copy can be fixed later if it has to be.

Jeff Bezos has a way of putting this that I think about a lot. Some decisions are one-way doors: once you go through, you can't easily come back, so you should be slow and careful. Most decisions are two-way doors, and because you can walk back through if it was wrong, you should make them quickly and move on.[^1]

The wording on a settings page is a two-way door, and so are the illustration on an empty state, the order of tabs in a sidebar nobody opens and the onboarding flow someone will see once. If they're good enough they should ship, because there's more to learn from people using them than from another week of polish.

## Where It Doesn't

The line I won't cross is the one people feel on contact: a button that lags, type that doesn't align with the thing next to it or a transition that eases out when everything else eases in. People treat those as polish to add at the end, but they're most of what the product feels like. I've refused to ship many times on parts like these, because there was still a bit more that could be done.

The one on the left is what "good enough" usually looks like in practice. It works and nothing is broken, but the count goes up a little after the press and the button doesn't react under the finger, so for a moment it's not clear the press registered. Nobody would file a bug for it.

The one on the right counts on the press and gives slightly as it does. It took two extra lines of CSS and the removal of a delay, but over a day of use the difference is enormous.

## How to Tell Which Is Which

When someone said something was good enough, I'd ask a few questions.

**Will people touch it every day?** That covers the main list, the primary button, the menu and the thing between two screens, and if the answer is yes, good enough isn't.

**Will they feel it before they can name it?** Lag, misalignment, inconsistent motion and type that sits a pixel high are all felt first and understood later, if at all. Those need to be right, because nobody will ever say they're wrong.

**Can we change it later without anyone noticing?** Copy, colours on a page nobody visits and the order of options in a rarely used dropdown all qualify, so those can ship and change once we know more.

**Is it holding something else up?** If waiting on this blocks the team, it should ship and we come back to it, but if it doesn't, there's no reason to rush the part people will touch most.

## It Adds Up

The hardest part is that each "good enough" is defensible on its own: one slow hover, one misaligned icon or one menu that eases differently from the others doesn't matter.

But people use the whole product rather than one hover, so forty small compromises become a product that feels slightly heavy, and nobody can point to why, so nobody fixes it. The team that made each call was right every time, but the result is still wrong.
