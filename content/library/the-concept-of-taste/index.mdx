People call taste subjective, and in one sense it is. You like what you like. But most of what feels like instinct was learned somewhere. You saw enough good work, and enough bad work, that some choices started to look right and others started to look off.

## What People Have Said

The argument is old. David Hume thought taste was a skill. Some people judge better than others, he wrote, because they've practised, compared a lot of work and kept their prejudices out of it.[^1]

Kant thought a judgment of taste starts with a feeling, not a rule. But when you call something beautiful, you don't mean it's beautiful to you. You talk as if everyone should agree.[^2]

Bourdieu was less generous. He found that what people liked tracked closely with their class and education, and argued that taste works as a way of marking who belongs where.[^3]

All three are partly right. Taste is personal, it can be learned, and it's shaped by the people around you.

## What It's Made Of

A lot of what reads as taste is structure you can name. Size, weight and space decide where you look first. Color sets a mood before you've read anything. A typeface sets the tone of a sentence before you've read the words.

When something feels just right, it usually isn't a mystery. Someone made a few of those decisions well and kept them consistent.

## It Moves

Taste changes. Baroque gave way to modernism, and modernism to postmodernism. Each one held for less time than the one before.

On the feed, a look can arrive and disappear in a few weeks. Platforms push whatever is already working, and everyone copies it until it's tired.

For product work, that means knowing the difference between a trend and a choice you'd make anyway. Hierarchy, spacing, contrast and readable type don't go out of date the way a gradient does.

## How to Build It

I think taste can be taught, or at least learned. You build it by looking at a lot of work and asking why some of it works.

Look widely. [Cosmos](https://www.cosmos.so), [Are.na](https://www.are.na), [Pinterest](https://www.pinterest.com) and [X](https://x.com) are a start, but so are films, records, buildings, clothes and old hardware. Things from outside your field are where the less obvious ideas come from.

Then compare. Put two things side by side, decide which is better and say why. The why is the part that sticks.

Leonardo da Vinci's notebooks are full of water, birds, machines and anatomy. He looked at everything and wrote down what he saw.

## Where It Ends Up

Over time the comparisons pile up. You stop reasoning through every choice, because you've already seen how it goes. When people say someone has taste, they usually mean that person has looked at enough to know.
