There's a difference between sharing what you made and performing the making of it. One is a record. The other is a show, and the show starts to steer the work.

I share a lot of what I build. I think it's good to. It's how I've met most of the people I like working with, and it's how a lot of us learn from each other. Posting is fine. What I'm interested in is what happens to the work when the post becomes the point.

## The Slide

Posting a detail you care about is fine. It forces you to look at it again, at a different size, with fresh eyes. Half the time I notice something I want to fix before I hit send.

The slide happens when you pick the detail because it will read well as a clip. Then the interface is being designed for the timeline, not for the person who has to use it on a Tuesday.

It's gradual. First you choose which thing to post. Then you choose which thing to build next based on what posted well. Then you're making the animation a bit longer, because a longer animation shows up better in a ten second video. Then the work has a second client, and it's the feed.

## What the Clip Shows

You can tell when a product was built to be screenshotted. The motion is longer than it needs to be. The empty state is a poster. The real states, the error, the loading, the third item in a list, are thin.

<StatesFigure />
<sub>The clip shows one state. People live in all of them.</sub>

A clip shows the best moment of the best state. Three items with nice names and good avatars, a smooth transition, a dark background. It almost never shows the list with nothing in it, the list while it loads, the error when the network drops, or the list with forty items and someone's name that wraps onto two lines.

Those states are where people spend most of their time. If you only polish what goes in the clip, you're polishing the part they'll see least.

## What the Timeline Rewards

The feed has its own taste, and it's not the same as the taste of someone using a product all day.

It rewards motion you can see at a glance, so things get slower and bigger. It rewards novelty, so the third tab switcher of the week has to do something the first two didn't. It rewards a single moment, so the work becomes a collection of moments instead of a product that holds together.

None of those are bad on their own. A slower animation is fine on a screen you see once. A novel interaction is fine if it solves something. But if they come from the timeline instead of from the work, they're decoration, and people feel the difference.

## Designing to Be Shared

There's an honest version of designing for sharing, and I think it matters.

When someone sends your product to a friend, what does the link look like? Does it open on the thing they meant to share, or the home page? If they take a screenshot of their own work in your tool, does it look good, or is it full of chrome and banners? Can they export something they're proud of?

That's designing something so it can be shared. It's for the person using it, and the sharing is theirs. It's the opposite of designing something so you can share it.

## A Test I Use

When I'm about to build or post something, I ask whether I'd build it this way if nobody would ever see it but the people using it.

If the answer is yes, posting it is just a record. If the answer is no, if the animation is longer than I'd keep it, or the state is one nobody will really hit, then I'm performing.

Then I watch it at real speed, one time, the way someone would see it in the middle of their day. Clips are watched on a loop. Products are used once, then again, then again. Something that looks great on a loop can feel slow the second time you're actually waiting for it.

<sub>On a loop, the longer one looks better. Used ten times in a day, it's four more seconds of waiting.</sub>

Build it for the people using it, then post it if you like. If it only feels good in the clip, it isn't finished.
