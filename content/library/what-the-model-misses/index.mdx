A model can hand over a working screen in an afternoon, with the layout, the components and a plausible colour already in place. That's new and it's useful, but it's also where a lot of products now stop.

I use these tools every day, so I'm not writing this to warn anyone off them. I'm writing it because the gap between what a model hands over and what's worth shipping has a shape, and once the shape is visible, it's much easier to close.

## What It Gets Right

A lot of what it does is actually good. A model is fast at structure, so it'll lay out a dashboard, wire up a form or build a settings page with all the right fields. It knows the common components and how they fit together, and it rarely forgets a label or a loading state it was asked for.

That used to be days of work, but now it's a first draft. I'd rather start from a working screen than an empty file, and I think most people would.

## What It Misses

What it misses is small and repeated: spacing that doesn't match the rest of the product, a hover state that scales when nothing else does, motion that plays every time because nobody told it not to and copy that sounds helpful but says nothing.

<SpacingFigure />
<sub>In the first result the gaps run 12, 20 and 9 and no two boxes share a height or a radius, and after the pass every gap is 16 and every box matches.</sub>

The first result usually has a gap of 12 here, 20 there and 9 somewhere else, with four corner radii on four cards and heights that are close but not equal. Any one of these would pass review, but together they make a screen that feels slightly unsteady, the way a room feels when one picture frame is crooked.

Motion goes the same way. A model will happily add an entrance animation to a card, a scale to a button and a bounce to a menu, each with its own duration and curve. Every one of them looks reasonable alone, but none of them were chosen with the others in mind.

<MotionFigure />
<sub>In the first result the card eases in and out over 600ms, the button overshoots over 300ms and the menu bounces over 500ms. After the pass the card's entrance is gone and the button and menu share one ease-out at 160ms.</sub>

Copy has the same problem, because models write in a voice that's confident, friendly and empty: "Unlock powerful insights" or "Seamlessly transform your workflow." It sounds like a product but says nothing about this one.

All of it is felt even when nobody can point at the line, so although the page works, nobody wants to stay on it.

<sub>The first card rises in at a tilt, with a gradient sparkle, a title-case headline and a pill button that grows on hover. After the pass it's a plain weekly report card that says three reports are ready, and its button gives slightly when pressed.</sub>

Nothing in the first version is broken, and plenty of teams would ship it. It has a gradient, a sparkle icon, title case, a button that grows on hover, an entrance that plays on every visit and padding that's different on every side. The words are about nothing in particular.

The second version is the same card with one radius and one padding, an icon on the same line as the title, a sentence that says what's actually there and a button that responds to a press instead of a hover. It has no entrance, because this is a card someone sees every day.

## How I Do the Pass

This is roughly the order I'd go in.

1. Delete first. I'd remove every animation, gradient and icon that wasn't doing a job, because it's much easier to see what's missing once the extra things are gone.
2. Check spacing against the scale. I'd go through every gap and every padding, and if the product used 8, 12, 16 and 24, a 9 or a 20 was a mistake, not a choice.
3. One curve per kind of motion. All the menus would share a timing and all the sheets would share another, and anything people saw constantly stayed still unless its motion explained something.
4. Read the copy out loud. If a line could have appeared on any product's landing page, I'd rewrite it to say what this product does.
5. Use it. I'd open the menu ten times, hover everything and go through the states, because the things that are wrong show up on the third or fourth use, never the first.

None of the steps is hard, but they take attention, applied in the same order every time.

## The Same First Result

When everyone has the same tools, the first result is the same for everyone: the same layouts, the same gradients and the same copy. Shipping the first result means shipping something that looks like everything else, because in a real sense it is everything else.

The pass is where taste gets applied: what to remove, what to retune and what should never have animated. I'm happy to let the model write the first draft, and for now I still do the pass myself before anything ships.
