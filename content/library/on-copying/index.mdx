I learned more from copying interfaces than from any course. By copying I don't mean posting the copies, but rebuilding something until I understood why each decision was made.

I'd open something I admired, like Linear, a system menu or a screen from my phone, and try to match it. My version would come out with the spacing wrong, the spring too bouncy and the type sitting a pixel high, and in fixing it I'd start to see.

## What You're Actually Copying

Copying gets a bad reputation because people stop at the surface, taking the radius and the shadow and calling it a style. That's the part a screenshot shows, so it's the part that gets taken.

<LayersFigure />
<sub>It starts as one surface labelled radius and shadow, pulls apart to show spacing, type and timing underneath, then closes up again.</sub>

Under the surface are three more layers: spacing (a scale someone chose and stuck to), type (a size and line height tuned against each other) and timing (a duration for every motion, and a reason one is shorter than the other). None of that is visible in a still, but all of it is why the thing feels the way it does.

A copy of the radius and shadow looks similar in a screenshot and feels nothing like the original in use, but a copy of the decisions doesn't even need to look the same.

## How I Do It

I'd pick one small thing I could rebuild in an evening, like a menu, a toggle or the way a list reorders.

Then I'd record the screen at full frame rate and play it back slowly, because most of what I was looking for happened in a few frames that I couldn't see at normal speed. I'd watch where it started moving from and whether the shadow came in with the shape or after it. I'd check whether the content faded in or was there from the first frame, and count the frames until it stopped.

<FramesFigure />
<sub>Over eight frames, about 117 milliseconds, the menu grows out of the button, the shadow arrives on the fourth frame and the content on the sixth.</sub>

I'd rebuild it side by side with the recording and adjust until I couldn't tell which was which. That was the slow part, because the first version was always close and always wrong: the spring a bit too loose, the menu growing from the centre instead of the corner or the text moving with the container when it should have stayed still.

The loop was the same, though: I'd change one thing, watch, compare and repeat, and each round I got a little better at seeing what was off before I changed anything.

After a while I stopped needing the reference, because I could look at something new and tell that the bounce was about 0.2, the exit was faster than the entrance and the shadow was two layers, which is what the copying was for.

## Where It Goes Wrong

There are two ways copying turns bad: stopping at the surface and passing the copy off as your own.

Stopping at the surface, which I've already covered, ends in a collection of borrowed styles that don't agree with each other, because the decisions underneath belonged to someone else.

Rebuilding something to learn from it is how most of us got good, but posting it without saying where it came from is something else. If I share a rebuild, I say what it's a rebuild of, because the person who made the original decision deserves the credit for it and saying so costs nothing.

## What You Keep

The point of copying is to take the decision apart, then put it down and use the lesson somewhere else.

When I rebuilt a menu, I wasn't collecting the menu but what it taught: that it grows from where the click landed, that it leaves faster than it arrives and that the shadow is softer than I'd have guessed. The next time I built something that opened from a button, in a completely different product, I'd start from those lessons instead of from zero.

For me, an hour of tuning a spring still teaches more than a week of reading about one, so rebuilding things is how I keep learning.
