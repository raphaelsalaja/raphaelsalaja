Most of what we know about motion on a screen started in film. In the 1930s, Disney's animators worked out how to make drawings feel alive, and Frank Thomas and Ollie Johnston later wrote those ideas down as the 12 principles of animation.[^1]

They were written for characters, not interfaces, but most of them carry over. Here's each one, and how I use it in a product.

## The Principles

### Squash and Stretch

Things squash when they land and stretch when they move fast, and how much they do it shows how heavy or soft they are.

In an interface, a little squash and stretch makes an element feel like it's reacting to whatever was just done to it.

<Demo>
  <SquashStretch />
</Demo>
<sub>Family's Wallet Icon Picker.[^2] An icon is picked from the row, and the new one lands squashed wide and flat, then wobbles back into its shape.</sub>

Here the wallet icon squashes and stretches as it changes, so it looks like it's turning into the new shape instead of being swapped for it.

I'd keep it small, because too much turns it cartoonish, and that goes for every principle here.

### Anticipation

Anticipation prepares someone for what's about to happen: a small movement before the main one, so the main one doesn't come out of nowhere.

In an interface, that might be a bell that wiggles before a badge appears, or a button that shrinks slightly while it's held. Select the issue below and hold to delete it.

<Demo>
  <Anticipation />
</Demo>

I'd save it for actions where a hint helps, like deleting something, and keep it short, because on every interaction it starts to feel like a gimmick and makes everything slower.

### Staging

Staging is about where the eye goes. Where anticipation is the moment before, staging is the moment during, and it makes sure the important thing is the thing people notice.

In practice it often means bringing things in one at a time: when a settings panel opens, the backdrop fades first, then the panel slides in, then the main control is highlighted.

<Demo>
  <Staging />
</Demo>

In this example, the backdrop blurs the page behind it, so there's only one thing left to look at.

Simple interactions rarely need staging, but complex ones usually do, because if everything animates at once, nobody knows where to look.

### Straight Ahead Action & Pose to Pose

There are two ways to animate a sequence. Straight ahead means drawing every frame in order, from first to last, which gives fluid and slightly unpredictable motion. Pose to pose means drawing the key poses first and filling in the frames between them afterwards, which gives more control.

Interfaces are pose to pose by nature: we define the states, and the browser fills in the frames between them. So the real choice is whether to show those in-between frames at all.

<Demo>
  <StraightAheadActionAndPoseToPose />
</Demo>

The first menu plays every frame, in and out, the second jumps straight between poses and the third skips the entrance but animates the exit.

How often something is used should decide which one to pick. On the Mac, context menus appear instantly and fade out when they close, which suits something opened so often that an entrance would start to feel slow.

### Follow Through & Overlapping Action

When something stops, parts of it keep going for a moment, the way a coat keeps swinging after the person wearing it stops, and when it starts, not every part moves at once.

In a list, that might mean each card arrives slightly after the one before it, so the list comes in as a cascade instead of a block.

<Demo>
  <FollowThroughAndOverlappingAction />
</Demo>
<sub>Dia's Split Tabs.[^3] Hovering the browser window slides two panes in from the sides. Dragging it towards one pulls that pane closer and tints it blue, and letting go springs the window back to the centre.</sub>

This example uses springs, which carry some of their speed past the target and settle back. I wrote about them in [When to Use a Spring](/library/when-to-use-a-spring), and Apple's [Animate with Springs](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10158) talk from WWDC23 is a good place to start.[^4]

I'd use overlap to soften starts and stops that feel abrupt and keep the delays short, because a long stagger makes a list feel slow to load.

### Slow In & Slow Out

Very little in the real world starts or stops instantly: a car speeds up from rest and slows down before it stops. Slow in and slow out means motion that does the same, easing into its speed and out of it again.

On screen, that's what easing curves are for, because linear motion, the same speed from start to finish, looks mechanical.

<Demo>
  <SlowInSlowOut />
</Demo>

These are a few common curves. [easing.dev](https://easing.dev) has many more, and lets you make your own.[^5]

The curve matters. I'd use `ease-out` for things entering the screen (they arrive fast and settle, so they feel responsive), `ease-in` for things leaving (they start gently and speed up on the way out) and `ease-in-out` for things moving from one place on screen to another.

### Arcs

Living things rarely move in straight lines: an arm swings in an arc, and a thrown ball curves. A gentle curve in an interface makes movement feel less mechanical for the same reason.

<Video src="/media/library/12-principles-of-animation/arcs.mp4" />
<sub>Apple's Dynamic Island.[^6] A music player is swiped away and curves up into the island, then grows back out of it to fill the screen.</sub>

Arcs are harder to use in everyday UI, where most things move along one axis, so they fit better in bigger moments, like bringing something forward on a landing page. They take a lot of experimenting to get right.

### Secondary Action

A secondary action supports the main one without competing with it.

After a form submits, the checkmark might pop and then sparkle briefly: the checkmark is the message, and the sparkle makes it feel good.

<Demo>
  <SecondaryAction />
</Demo>

Sound can be a secondary action too. Games do this all the time: a hit lands with a sound and a flash together, and each makes the other feel stronger. A short, quiet click on a toggle does the same job in an interface.

### Timing

Timing is how long an action takes, and it decides whether an interface feels quick or slow, deliberate or chaotic.

A tooltip can feel right at 150 milliseconds and annoying at 400, so I keep most interactions under 300 milliseconds.

<Demo>
  <Timing />
</Demo>

Similar actions should take similar times, so I'd pick a few durations and reuse them across the product. I wrote more about this in [A Note on Timing](/library/a-note-on-timing).

### Exaggeration

Exaggeration pushes a motion past what's realistic so it's hard to miss. In an interface, it's for feedback someone needs to notice.

An invalid form field is a good example. Instead of only turning red it shakes, which no field does in real life, but nobody misses it.

<Demo>
  <Exaggeration />
</Demo>

I'd use it sparingly, for moments where someone should feel something or notice something important, which tends to mean errors, confirmations, empty states and onboarding.

### Solid Drawing

In traditional animation, solid drawing means making things look three-dimensional, with weight and volume, sitting in a space.

In an interface, that usually means shadows, layering and a little perspective, used to show what's on top and what can be touched.

<Demo>
  <SolidDrawing />
</Demo>
<sub>Linear Orbit.[^7] A dot orbits a spinning dashed ring in 3D until Pause is pressed, then the ring closes, the centre fills orange and the dot drifts to rest in front, turning purple.</sub>

3D transforms in CSS need `perspective`, which controls how far the object is from the viewer, and without it a rotation looks flat. This example uses it.

Depth has to stay consistent: an icon that rotates in 3D should keep its depth the whole way round, because if it suddenly flattens or flips the wrong way, the illusion breaks.

### Appeal

Appeal is whether people like looking at something and using it, and it's the gap between an interface that works and one people choose.

</Demo>
<sub>A few people making nice things on the web.[^8] Their clips sit in a stack, and each scroll blurs the front one away and brings the next one forward.</sub>

The products people keep going back to usually come down to a lot of small things: details someone cared about, interactions that are still fun the tenth time and a product that seems to know what they want next. Most of that is the other eleven principles done well.

## Putting It Together

A lot of software ships fast now and a lot of it looks the same, so motion someone has thought about is one of the few things that still makes a product feel different.

The principles help avoid both extremes: motion so sparse the interface feels dead and motion so heavy it gets in the way. Somewhere between them is enough animation, of the right kind, in the right places.

Done well, most of it goes unnoticed, and people just say the app feels nice to use.
