People spend a lot of time picking an easing curve and almost no time picking a duration, so they'll compare four cubic-beziers side by side and then type 300ms because it's the number everyone types.

The curve is the shape, but the duration is whether the motion belongs in the product at all. A perfect curve at the wrong duration still feels wrong, but a plain ease-out at the right duration usually feels fine.

## Duration

Try the same menu at three speeds, opening and closing it a few times at each one before you decide.

</Demo>
<sub>The menu opens and closes under the button in 150ms, 300ms or 600ms, on the same curve over the same distance each time.</sub>

At 150 milliseconds the menu is just there, so you notice that it moved but not how. At 300 it looks nice the first time, but by the fifth time you're waiting for it. At 600 every open is a small pause, and the product starts to feel slow even though nothing is loading.

Any of these could be right somewhere, but for a menu only the first one is, and the same 300 milliseconds is fine for a sheet opened twice a session.

## Frequency

The rule I use most is simple: the more often someone sees a motion, the shorter it should be.

<FrequencyFigure />
<sub>From seen once to seen all day, the line falls from onboarding at 600ms through a page at 400, a sheet at 300, a toast at 240 and a menu at 180 to a hover at 100.</sub>

A screen seen once, like onboarding, can take its time, because it's the only time anyone will see it and a slower entrance gives them a second to take in where they are. A page transition seen a few times an hour sits somewhere around 300 to 400 milliseconds, a sheet or a dialog around 300, a toast a bit less, a menu opened constantly 150 to 200 and a hover 100 or so.

At the far end, things triggered from the keyboard dozens of times an hour often shouldn't animate at all. Someone who opens a command menu with a shortcut already knows where it's going to be, so the animation is only in the way.

These are only starting points, so I'd tune from there: the values change with the product, but the shape of the curve, falling as frequency rises, almost never does.

## In and Out

The second thing people miss is that entrances and exits don't have to match, and most of the time they shouldn't.

<AsymmetryFigure />
<sub>The top line draws the menu opening over 200ms, and the bottom line draws it closing over 120ms in a little more than half the time.</sub>

When something arrives, the motion is doing a job, because it shows where the menu came from and what it's attached to. When it leaves, the person has already made a choice by picking an item or clicking away, so making them watch it leave at the same speed it arrived feels like being asked to wait for a door to close.

I'd usually make exits a bit more than half the entrance, so a menu that opened in 200 milliseconds closed in about 120. Some things don't animate out at all, and a context menu that vanishes the moment something is picked feels more responsive, not less.

## Distance

Duration should grow a little with distance, but not in proportion. Something moving across the whole screen needs more time than a tooltip moving four pixels, or it looks like it teleported. But double the distance doesn't need double the time, because if it did, a full-screen sheet would take a second.

I think of it as a floor and a ceiling: nothing in an interface should take less than about 100 milliseconds if people are meant to see it move, and very little should take more than 500 unless it's the only thing happening on screen.

## How I Time Things

When I was matching something that already existed, a system animation or a reference from another product, I'd time it against a recording. I'd record the screen, step through it frame by frame and count the frames from first movement to rest, and at 60 frames per second, twelve frames is 200 milliseconds.

Then I'd map that onto a spring by duration and nudge it until it stopped calling attention to itself. That last part was the real test: I'd watch it three times, and if I still noticed the animation the third time through, it was too slow, or too proud of itself.

When there was no reference, I'd start fast and slow down until it read, instead of starting slow and speeding up. Starting slow, everything looks smooth and nothing feels too long yet, but starting fast finds the shortest duration that still explains what happened, and that's usually the right one.

## What It's For

Motion should explain what changed: a row left, a sheet arrived or a value updated. After that, it should disappear.

The interfaces that feel expensive are usually the ones where most actions are shorter than people expect, so the timing goes unnoticed and nobody is left waiting. That's what I aim for when I time things, even though the numbers change with every product.
